Derivation of the Linear Isotropic Stress–Strain Relation

The target result is

\begin{equation} \bm{\sigma} = \lambda\,\tr(\bm{\varepsilon})\,\mathbf{I} + 2\mu\,\bm{\varepsilon}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \sigma_{ij} = \lambda\,\varepsilon_{kk}\,\delta_{ij} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{ij}. \label{eq:target} \end{equation}

Assumptions: the material is linear (small strains), isotropic (the same in all directions), and has an elastic energy that depends only on the strain.

Setup: the strain tensor

For a displacement field \(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\),

\begin{equation} \varepsilon_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right), \qquad \varepsilon_{ij}=\varepsilon_{ji}. \end{equation}

The symmetric part is kept because the antisymmetric part is a rigid rotation, which does not deform the material. The trace

\begin{equation} \tr\bm{\varepsilon} = \varepsilon_{kk} = \varepsilon_{11}+\varepsilon_{22}+\varepsilon_{33} \end{equation}

measures the relative volume change.

Derivation 1: from the elastic energy

Step 1: stress from energy.

Let \(\Psi(\bm{\varepsilon})\) be the elastic energy per unit volume. Define

\begin{equation} \sigma_{ij} = \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial \varepsilon_{ij}}, \end{equation}

the 3D analogue of \(F=-dU/dx\) for a spring.

Step 2: Ψ is quadratic.

Linear elasticity means \(\sigma\) is linear in \(\varepsilon\). Since the derivative of a quadratic form is linear, \(\Psi\) must be quadratic in \(\varepsilon\). (There is no constant or linear term: the unstrained state has zero energy and zero stress.)

Step 3: Ψ is rotation invariant.

For an isotropic material, \(\Psi\) must be a scalar that does not change under a rotation of coordinates, \(\bm{\varepsilon}\to \mathbf{R}\bm{\varepsilon}\mathbf{R}^{T}\). The only quadratic rotation invariants of a symmetric tensor are

\begin{equation} (\tr\bm{\varepsilon})^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \tr(\bm{\varepsilon}^2)=\varepsilon_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}. \end{equation}

Hence the most general form has two constants,

\begin{equation} \Psi = \frac{1}{2}\lambda\,(\tr\bm{\varepsilon})^2 + \mu\,\varepsilon_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}. \label{eq:psi} \end{equation}

This defines the Lamé parameters \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\).

Step 4: differentiate.

Treating the components of \(\bm{\varepsilon}\) as independent,

\begin{align} \frac{\partial (\tr\bm{\varepsilon})^2}{\partial \varepsilon_{ij}} &= 2\,\tr(\bm{\varepsilon})\,\frac{\partial \varepsilon_{kk}}{\partial \varepsilon_{ij}} = 2\,\tr(\bm{\varepsilon})\,\delta_{ij}, \\ \frac{\partial (\varepsilon_{kl}\varepsilon_{kl})}{\partial \varepsilon_{ij}} &= 2\,\varepsilon_{ij}. \end{align}

Therefore

\begin{equation} \sigma_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\lambda\cdot 2\,\tr(\bm{\varepsilon})\,\delta_{ij} + \mu\cdot 2\,\varepsilon_{ij} = \lambda\,\tr(\bm{\varepsilon})\,\delta_{ij} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{ij}, \end{equation}

which is Eq. \(\eqref{eq:target}\). The factor 2 in front of \(\mu\) comes from differentiating the square.

Derivation 2: from symmetry alone

Step 1: most general linear law.

\begin{equation} \sigma_{ij} = C_{ijkl}\,\varepsilon_{kl}, \end{equation}

where \(C_{ijkl}\) has \(3^4 = 81\) components.

Step 2: reduce the number of constants.

  • \(\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}\) and \(\varepsilon_{kl}=\varepsilon_{lk}\) give \(C_{ijkl}=C_{jikl}=C_{ijlk}\), leaving 36 constants.
  • Existence of an energy, \(C_{ijkl}=\partial^2\Psi/\partial\varepsilon_{ij}\partial\varepsilon_{kl}=C_{klij}\), leaves 21 constants (the most general anisotropic crystal).
  • Isotropy requires \(C_{ijkl}\) to take the same form in every rotated frame.

Step 3: isotropic rank-4 tensors.

The only rank-4 tensors invariant under all rotations are built from \(\delta_{ij}\):

\begin{equation} C_{ijkl} = \alpha\,\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \beta\,\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \gamma\,\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}. \end{equation}

The symmetry \(C_{ijkl}=C_{ijlk}\) forces \(\beta=\gamma\). Renaming \(\alpha=\lambda\) and \(\beta=\gamma=\mu\),

\begin{equation} C_{ijkl} = \lambda\,\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \mu\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\right). \end{equation}

Step 4: contract with the strain.

\begin{align} \sigma_{ij} = C_{ijkl}\,\varepsilon_{kl} &= \lambda\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta_{kl}\varepsilon_{kl} + \mu\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\varepsilon_{kl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\varepsilon_{kl}\right) \nonumber\\ &= \lambda\,\delta_{ij}\,\varepsilon_{kk} + \mu\left(\varepsilon_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ji}\right) \nonumber\\ &= \lambda\,\tr(\bm{\varepsilon})\,\delta_{ij} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{ij}, \end{align}

using \(\varepsilon_{ji}=\varepsilon_{ij}\). Again Eq. \(\eqref{eq:target}\).

Check: μ is the shear modulus

Apply a simple shear with only \(\varepsilon_{12}=\varepsilon_{21}=\gamma/2\) nonzero, where \(\gamma\) is the change in angle. Then \(\tr\bm{\varepsilon}=0\) and

\begin{equation} \sigma_{12} = 2\mu\,\frac{\gamma}{2} = \mu\,\gamma . \end{equation}

Shear stress equals \(\mu\) times the shear angle, so \(\mu\) is the shear modulus.

Volumetric–deviatoric split

Decompose the strain into a trace part and a traceless (deviatoric) part:

\begin{equation} \bm{\varepsilon} = \bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{dev}} + \frac{1}{3}\tr(\bm{\varepsilon})\,\mathbf{I}, \qquad \tr\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{dev}} = 0 . \end{equation}

Then

\begin{equation} \varepsilon_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij} = \bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{dev}}:\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{dev}} + \frac{1}{3}(\tr\bm{\varepsilon})^2, \end{equation}

and substituting into Eq. \(\eqref{eq:psi}\) gives

\begin{equation} \Psi = \frac{1}{2}\,K\,(\tr\bm{\varepsilon})^2 + \mu\,\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{dev}}:\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{dev}}, \qquad K = \lambda + \frac{2}{3}\mu . \end{equation}

So an isotropic solid resists volume change through the bulk modulus \(K\) and shape change through \(\mu\).

Relation to E and ν

\begin{equation} \lambda = \frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}, \qquad \mu = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)} . \end{equation}
